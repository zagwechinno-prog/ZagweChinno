Why I build systems

I built a business for almost six years.
Then I watched it disappear in a single day.

No warning letter. No formal notice. No time to prepare. Just officials in our office. Phones taken. Licence taken. Currencies taken. A business we had spent almost six years building was being dismantled in front of us.

That day changed how I think about business. But I didn't come to systems architecture from theory — I came to it through building, losing, and starting again.

In 2014, a co-founder and I started a bureau de change and spent almost six years inside the machinery of international business: currencies, capital, regulation, risk, cross-border transactions. I thought I understood the rules. Then the Central Bank shut us down in an afternoon, with no process and no warning. What had taken six years to build was gone in hours.

A business does not exist by itself. It operates inside systems it does not control.

That was the moment one of my deepest assumptions broke. I had believed that if you understood the market, worked hard, and built carefully, you could protect what you built. I was wrong — and the question that replaced it stayed with me: what does a business actually depend on? Where are the constraints, and what happens when one assumption changes?

I moved into e-commerce, learned digital marketing the hard way, and later worked across dozens of businesses at a digital marketing agency — which gave me something operating one company never could: pattern recognition. Good people still struggled. Good technology still produced bad outcomes. The problem was rarely the thing everyone was looking at. It was the system connecting the things underneath it.

In 2024 I taught myself Python and moved into machine learning — language models, sentiment analysis, and eventually the moment ChatGPT stopped looking like a tool and started looking like infrastructure. Years of watching where businesses break, meeting a technology that could change how businesses actually operate.

Not "how can I use AI?" — but "what happens when intelligence becomes infrastructure?"

That's where Quelx came from — an AI development company built on one principle: start with the business, find the constraint, then build the system. Resilience isn't having a plan for when things go wrong. It's building a business that can adapt when the environment changes.

I didn't start with AI. I started with a business. I lost it. I started again. I learned the system. That is why I build systems.

How I work

The operating sequence

Across every venture — finance, brand, commerce, AI — the same six moves produce the outcome.

  1. 01 Diagnose Find the actual problem. Separate the symptom from the cause. Locate where money, time, or attention is being lost.
  2. 02 Architect Design the system that should exist — not a patch on the system that's already wrong.
  3. 03 Encode Turn judgment and process that live only in someone's head into logic, documentation, and workflow.
  4. 04 Integrate Connect people, data, technology, operations, and finance. A system isn't a system while its parts stay disconnected.
  5. 05 Automate Remove unnecessary human intervention — after the process is correct, never before.
  6. 06 Scale Design for leverage: growth without a proportional increase in labor, cost, or founder involvement.
